# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Methods, Evidence, and Open Questions

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a practical way to improve LLM reasoning when tasks provide checkable answers. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) removes a separately trained critic and compares multiple sampled answers for a prompt; DeepSeekMath reported improvements on GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH with this setup. [1]
- DeepSeek-R1-Zero demonstrated a prominent RL-first recipe: GRPO applied to a base model using rule-based outcome and format rewards, while the fuller R1 pipeline added cold-start data, rejection sampling, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and further RL. The authors also report readability, language-mixing, and task-generalization limitations. [2][3]
- Reward design is a central bottleneck. Outcome rewards are cheap when answer checking is reliable but provide sparse credit; process rewards can localize feedback, yet their labels and learned verifiers may be noisy or exploitable. [4][5][6]
- Online policy-gradient RL is only one point in the design space: offline RL, iterative preference optimization, and test-time search/distillation can reduce or relocate training costs, but depend on data coverage, verifiers, or additional inference compute. [7][8][9]
- Reported gains do not settle whether RL expands a model’s reasoning repertoire or mainly improves sampling efficiency, and comparison is complicated by reproducibility, run-to-run variance, and safety concerns. [10][11][12][13][14]

## Background
“RL for reasoning” covers several related but distinct procedures: generating trajectories from a language-model policy, scoring them with an outcome or process signal, and updating the policy (or using the score to select future outputs). It is not synonymous with all reasoning post-training. For example, self-generated correct rationales can be filtered and used for SFT, and preference optimization can train from chosen/rejected pairs without the same online policy-gradient loop. [2][7][8]

A useful conceptual split is between *where feedback comes from* and *how it changes the model*. Verifiable-reward RL (RLVR) uses executable tests, answer matching, or other rule-based checks where available. Learned reward or process models extend beyond deterministic checks but can be miscalibrated. Meanwhile, test-time scaling searches or selects among outputs at inference without necessarily updating the policy parameters. [2][5][7]

## 1. From PPO-style optimization to GRPO and R1-style training

DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO as a PPO variant that avoids a separately learned value/critic model. For each problem, it samples a group of responses and uses their rewards to form a relative baseline; clipped policy updates and a KL regularizer constrain the update relative to a reference policy. The stated motivation is lower memory and compute than training a value model. In its reported DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B setup, GRPO improved GSM8K from 82.9% to 88.2%, MATH from 46.8% to 51.7%, and CMATH from 84.6% to 88.8%. These are setup-specific results, not general guarantees. [1]

R1-Zero made the “RL-first” pattern visible at larger scale: it applied GRPO to DeepSeek-V3-Base without prior SFT. The paper reports AIME 2024 pass@1 increasing from 15.6% to 71.0% after RL, with majority-vote consistency@64 of 86.7%. Its reward uses rule-based correctness checks for math and code, plus a format reward; code examples are assessed through compilation and test cases. The authors avoid neural outcome/process reward models in the R1-Zero stage, citing reward-hacking risk, compute, and pipeline complexity. [2]

The full R1 recipe is not simply “RL instead of supervision.” It incorporates cold-start data, reasoning-oriented RL, rejection sampling to collect reasoning traces for SFT, and another RL phase; general-domain data can receive model-based rewards. The published account describes language-consistency reward as one intervention. This multistage arrangement reflects a practical compromise: RL can elicit and reinforce successful trajectories, while curated demonstrations help shape readability and broaden the training signal. [2][3]

Algorithmic variants address optimization pathologies rather than removing dependence on reward quality. DAPO reports an open-source training system and identifies entropy collapse and reward noise in an initial GRPO run as motivations for design changes; on Qwen2.5-32B it reports 50 points on AIME 2024 using half the training steps of its cited R1-Zero-Qwen-32B baseline. Such comparisons are tied to each paper’s model, setup, and benchmark protocol. [11] More generally, reproduction work notes that inconsistent initializations, data, and experimental settings can explain apparently conflicting findings about RL techniques. [12]

## 2. Reward design: outcome signals, process feedback, and verifier limits

Outcome reward models (ORMs) score a completed solution, often for final correctness. For math or code this may be practical when answers can be matched or programs run against tests; it avoids asking a learned model to judge every intermediate inference. Its weakness is sparse credit assignment: a final pass/fail signal gives little direct information about which reasoning step helped or failed. R1’s reliance on rule-based verification in math/code illustrates the attractive case, while its authors explicitly caution that reliable rules are difficult for open-ended writing and model-judged rewards can be exploited. [2][3]

Process supervision attempts to score intermediate steps. OpenAI’s process-supervision study distinguishes step-level feedback from outcome supervision and reports that, in its MATH Best-of-N experiments, a process-supervised reward model selected more correct solutions than an outcome-supervised one; it also cautions that the generality beyond math is unknown. [4] A later approach, process advantage verifiers (PAVs), scores the change in estimated probability of eventual correctness attributable to a step, rather than merely labeling a step right or wrong. Its authors report improvements in test-time search and online RL sample efficiency over ORM-based comparisons, while noting dependence on good prover policies and sensitivity to verifier fitting errors. [5]

The evidence also argues against treating a process reward as ground truth. ProcessBench offers 3,400 human-annotated cases focused on identifying the earliest erroneous step. The paper reports that evaluated PRMs can generalize poorly from GSM8K/MATH to more difficult OlympiadBench and Omni-MATH examples, and may underperform prompted general-model critics. It also explains how Monte Carlo labels can be noisy because a completion model may arrive at the right final answer after a flawed step, or fail despite a sound local step. [6] Thus outcome accuracy, process-error identification, and generalization should be evaluated separately rather than collapsed into one score. [4][6]

Reward hacking and reward-model misspecification remain central risks. DeepSeek-R1’s published analysis describes model-based preference rewards being hacked in a later stage and observes that longer responses may become inefficient. [3] Work on process-reward credit assignment reports that changing from summation-form to min-form credit assignment and mixing a small proportion of verifiable-reward examples mitigated observed PRM hacking, but did not eliminate it. This supports a conservative design principle: combine signals and audit behavior, rather than assuming a denser learned reward is inherently safer or more faithful. [3][5]

## 3. What the gains mean: exploration, coverage, and sample efficiency

A positive result on pass@1 does not by itself show that RL has created reasoning strategies absent from the pretrained model. A cross-model analysis of RLVR reports better low-k performance but finds that base models can outperform RL-trained models at large sampling budgets; it interprets the tested results as improved sampling efficiency over paths already present in the base distribution, with reasoning coverage narrowing during training. The authors explicitly bound this conclusion to tested models, benchmarks, and sampling setups. [10]

Other results show that the data and exploration regime can strongly affect apparent sample efficiency. A one-example RLVR study reports that a selected single math problem raised Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B MATH500 from 36.0% to 73.6%, comparable to the reported MATH500 result from a 1.2k-example subset in that study; it also notes that two examples slightly exceeded the single-example result. The findings are tied to selected examples and configurations, and the authors’ ablations emphasize policy-gradient learning and appropriately weighted entropy. This is striking evidence that training-example selection can matter, not evidence that arbitrary one-example training is sufficient. [15]

These findings motivate evaluation beyond a single pass@1 number: compare pass@k curves, base-model sampling, solution coverage, and compute at both training and inference. High-k behavior asks whether RL improves the probability of finding known successful paths or increases the diversity of usable reasoning; the available evidence does not support a universal answer. [15][10]

## 4. Alternatives and complements to online policy-gradient RL

Offline methods learn from fixed trajectories or preference pairs and can avoid repeated on-policy rollout generation, but the fit depends on what the dataset covers. OREO, an offline RL approach for multistep reasoning, learns a policy and value function through a soft Bellman objective and reports gains over offline baselines on GSM8K, MATH, and ALFWorld; its value function can also guide test-time tree search. [7] DPO-style studies report benchmark improvements and lower computational overhead in particular setups, including iterative DPO variants that collect fresh generations and use verifiable feedback over repeated rounds. The latter is better viewed as an online-data/preference-optimization hybrid than as a fixed offline replacement for RL. [8]

Theoretical work on preference fine-tuning highlights data coverage as a key dividing line: offline contrastive methods require global coverage for convergence under the analyzed assumptions, whereas online RL can work under weaker partial-coverage conditions. The work proposes combining offline preferences with online data, and reports better performance than pure DPO in its experiments. The general implication is conditional: offline optimization is attractive when data is adequate, while online interaction can expose the learner to policy-relevant regions missing from a static preference set. [9]

Distillation and self-training shift feedback into data construction. R1’s rejection-sampled traces are used for SFT, and its paper reports distilled dense models trained on teacher-generated examples without an RL stage; it reports direct distillation from R1 outperforming RL on Qwen2.5-32B in its comparison. Earlier self-training approaches likewise filter generated rationales by answer correctness and train on retained traces, but can stall when the model cannot solve new examples. These procedures can transfer or reinforce reasoning behaviors, but they are not equivalent to direct online reward optimization of the student. [2][7]

Test-time scaling (TTS) spends compute during inference to generate, search, and verify candidates; unlike policy-gradient RL, it need not alter model parameters. Verifiers may score final outcomes or intermediate processes, so the training/inference distinction is about when compute is applied, not whether a reward-like function exists. In practice, TTS can complement RL, but moves cost to inference and inherits verifier reliability limits. [5][7]

## 5. Evaluation, systems, and safety

Reproducibility is a substantive methodological problem. DAPO releases its algorithm, code, and curated data in response to the difficulty of reproducing results with undisclosed training details. [11] A systematic study of RL techniques attributes inconsistent conclusions in part to differences in experimental settings, data, and initialization, and argues for controlled, context-specific evaluation rather than ranking techniques from mismatched runs. [12]

Reasoning quality is also stochastic and costly. ReasonBENCH argues single-run accuracy conceals variation under stochastic decoding and evaluates both quality and cost across multiple runs; it reports substantial instability across tasks and strategies, including wide confidence intervals for methods with similar apparent performance. This suggests reporting distributions and resource use—not only best scores—when comparing RL-trained reasoners. [13] Systems constraints feed back into algorithm design: the R1 account says long software-engineering evaluation times restricted large-scale RL for those tasks, underscoring that a theoretically useful reward is not enough if it is too expensive to obtain at training scale. [3]

Safety evaluation must include reasoning traces and model adaptation, not only final answers. A safety assessment of R1 reports jailbreak vulnerabilities and cautions that public weights may be fine-tuned in ways that compromise protections; it also finds safety concerns in reasoning traces. These results are specific to the assessment’s models and tests, but they make clear that optimizing reasoning capability and optimizing safe behavior are not automatically the same objective. [3][14]

## Trends and open problems

1. **Verifiers are the effective boundary of RLVR.** More tasks become trainable when correctness can be checked cheaply and reliably; open-ended domains still require learned judges or human feedback, which raise calibration, bias, and hacking concerns. How to make verifiers robust under policy shift remains unresolved. [2][3][5][6]
2. **Credit assignment is still contested.** Outcome rewards are simple but sparse; process rewards promise localization yet depend on annotation quality, policy-dependent labels, and process-level evaluation. A robust recipe may need mixed signals and diagnostics that measure both final correctness and intermediate validity. [4][5][6]
3. **Exploration and coverage deserve first-class metrics.** The gap between low-k gains and high-k behavior, alongside evidence that selected examples and entropy affect learning, motivates explicit measurement of solution diversity, coverage, and the marginal value of additional rollout compute. [15][10]
4. **No single post-training family dominates every constraint.** Online RL can acquire fresh policy-conditioned data; offline preference optimization can save rollout costs where coverage is sufficient; distillation and self-training can transfer successful traces; TTS trades inference budget for search. Hybrid pipelines are likely to be judged by total data, compute, verifier reliability, and deployment latency—not benchmark accuracy alone. [2][7][8][9]
5. **Evaluation and alignment must scale with capability.** Better variance-aware reporting, reproducible training artifacts, lower-cost verifiers for code/interactive tasks, and safety assessments of both traces and outputs are central open requirements. Current evidence identifies these needs, but does not yet establish general solutions. [3][11][12][13][14]
