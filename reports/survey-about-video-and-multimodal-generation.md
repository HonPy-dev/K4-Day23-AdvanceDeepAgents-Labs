# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Control, Evaluation, and Open Challenges

## TL;DR
- Video generation is not one architectural recipe: latent diffusion Transformers model continuous spacetime representations, while discrete-token autoregressive systems offer a shared sequence interface for video, text, images, and audio; hybrid methods seek parallel refinement without strict next-token decoding everywhere. [1][2][3][4]
- Progress depends on representation and data pipeline as well as backbone: temporal compression reduces sequence cost, but an aggressive setting can impair convergence; curated and staged training is a documented scaling strategy. [5][6]
- Multimodal control has expanded beyond text to reference frames, trajectories, camera poses, masks, and audio, but individual methods address selected control dimensions rather than guaranteeing all forms of consistency together. [7][8][9]
- Visual quality alone does not establish coherent motion, physical plausibility, or alignment with user intent; benchmarks measure these separately and have meaningful coverage and sampling limitations. [10][11][12]
- Audio-video generation is an emerging joint-generation and evaluation problem: recent work separates localized event/lip timing from global visual mood, while benchmarks identify cross-modal coherence as unresolved. [13][14]

## Background
Generative video is a sequence modeling problem with a steep representational cost: a clip contains spatial structure at each frame and temporal dependencies across frames. A common route compresses video into a lower-dimensional latent, then applies diffusion or Transformer operations to spatiotemporal tokens. Another route converts frames or clips into discrete tokens and predicts them autoregressively, often within a multimodal sequence model. These choices shape fidelity, efficiency, and how image, text, audio, and structured control conditions can be combined. [1][2][3][4]

The evidence surveyed here comes from primary-paper records and project or benchmark reports. Reported performance is task- and benchmark-specific; it should not be read as a single leaderboard ranking across different datasets, prompts, resolutions, or evaluation protocols. Sora’s public report, for example, describes representation choices while omitting implementation details; some paper abstracts also summarize comparative results without publishing scores in the abstract. [1][2][10]

## 1. Architectures: continuous diffusion, discrete tokens, and hybrids

### Latent diffusion and spacetime Transformers
Latent-diffusion systems reduce sequence length before the main generative model processes a clip. Latte uses a pretrained variational autoencoder (VAE) to encode videos, extracts spatiotemporal tokens, and models their distribution with Transformer blocks; its paper studies variants that decompose spatial and temporal structure. [2] Sora’s technical report similarly describes spatial and temporal compression followed by spacetime patches for a diffusion Transformer. One representation accommodates images and variable-sized videos by treating an image as a one-frame video and varying the patch grid. The report says sample quality improves with training compute, but omits implementation details and does not establish a general quantitative scaling law. [1]

Other systems make explicit efficiency and transfer choices. Open-Sora factorizes attention across within-frame spatial and across-frame temporal dimensions, initializes from an image-generation DiT, and adds temporal attention; it also describes a 3D autoencoder with temporal compression. [3] CogVideoX combines a 3D causal VAE with a diffusion Transformer and joint text/video processing, and reports progressive training and multi-resolution packing. Its paper reports an ablation in which an excessively aggressive compression setting made convergence extremely difficult. [5] These studies point to a coupled design problem: latent efficiency must be balanced against preserving trainable spatial and temporal information; more compression is not automatically better. [3][5]

### Autoregressive and hybrid generation
VideoPoet uses a decoder-only autoregressive Transformer over multimodal inputs and a mixture of generative objectives, with task-specific adaptation for video generation and related tasks. Its paper describes processing image, video, text, and audio; the project’s representation uses MAGVIT V2 for image/video tokens and SoundStream for audio tokens in a unified vocabulary. A separate non-autoregressive high-resolution upsampler illustrates sequence-length pressures at higher resolution. [4] LARP addresses tokenization directly: learned holistic queries pool video patches into discrete tokens, and the token count can vary to trade sequence length against reconstruction quality. [6]

A hybrid line combines autoregressive temporal organization with parallel denoising of visual tokens. Lumos-1 uses autoregressive structure across frames and masked discrete diffusion within frames, with a masking policy designed to reduce the latency of strict next-token decoding. Its reported comparisons are specific to its benchmarks. [7] These approaches occupy complementary points in the design space: diffusion supports parallel denoising; autoregressive token models provide a shared sequence interface for multimodal tasks; hybrids attempt to exploit both. The available sources do not establish a universally superior family. [1][4][7]

## 2. Data, compression, and scaling recipes

Scaling is not only a matter of model size. Stable Video Diffusion reports a three-stage recipe—text-to-image pretraining, low-resolution video pretraining on a large dataset, then high-resolution fine-tuning on a smaller high-quality set—and studies data curation. The authors report that curated data improved preference results at multiple data scales and that a larger curated subset outperformed a much smaller one at matched training steps. Their assembled pretraining dataset contains 152 million examples; these are results from that study, not a universal scaling law. [8]

Representation compression, training curriculum, and data quality interact. Open-Sora describes a 3D autoencoder and factorized attention, while CogVideoX reports progressive training and multi-resolution frame packing alongside VAE compression. [3][5] VideoUFO provides a dataset-building example focused on user prompts: it reports more than 1.09 million clips paired with brief and detailed captions, gathered from Creative Commons-labeled YouTube videos and checked for topic relevance using captions. Its authors note that visually verifying every clip was not feasible and that caption quality affects analyses of dataset coverage. [9] Dataset size can expand coverage, but collection and filtering determine what that scale represents; it is not equivalent to verified semantic diversity or uniform caption accuracy. [8][9]

## 3. Multimodal conditioning and controllability

Text remains a major interface, but useful video control often requires specifying appearance and motion more explicitly. VideoComposer composes text with spatial and temporal signals, including motion vectors, sketches, and reference video. MotionCtrl uses separate modules for camera-pose sequences and object trajectories, so camera and object motion can be controlled independently or together. Motion Prompting uses sparse or dense point trajectories to control camera and object motion, including visibility and occlusion. These methods make motion explicit, although their cited abstracts do not provide a common quantitative measure of control fidelity. [10][11][12]

Reference and editing conditions address another problem: retaining selected content while changing motion or other elements. Moonshot combines image and text conditioning and describes optional geometry control via pretrained ControlNet modules. ConsistI2V focuses on image-to-video continuity, using first-frame spatiotemporal attention and low-frequency noise initialization to preserve layout and appearance; it reports automatic and human evaluations on its proposed I2V-Bench. [13][14] VACE unifies reference-to-video, video-to-video editing, and masked video editing through a condition unit and context adapter. The reported capabilities show increasing compositionality, but do not establish that arbitrary controls can be combined without interference. [15]

Multimodal conditions can encode sound and identity as well. HuMo combines text, reference images, and audio for human-centric video; it injects reference-image latents and integrates audio features through cross-attention, with progressive training intended to balance subject preservation and synchronization. Its authors identify scarce aligned multimodal training data and interference among control objectives as challenges. [16] The broader frontier is thus not merely adding more inputs: it is acquiring aligned examples, disentangling control signals, and testing whether all requested properties hold simultaneously. [16]

## 4. Temporal consistency, physical plausibility, and evaluation

Temporal quality is not a single attribute. VBench organizes evaluation into 16 dimensions, including identity consistency, motion smoothness, flickering, and spatial relationships, with tailored prompts, automated measures, and human-preference annotations. [17] DEVIL emphasizes dynamics: whether motion range and intensity match the prompt as well as whether motion is continuous. Its authors report up to about 90% correlation with human ratings in their evaluation, while noting limited model coverage and the need for finer-grained dynamics grades. These frameworks complement visual-quality measures: an attractive frame sequence may still fail to enact requested movement. [18]

Long-range consistency and physical commonsense are distinct challenges. Temporally Consistent Transformers identifies cases where a model may invent different content after an object or scene leaves view and later returns; its proposed benchmarks target long-horizon consistency in procedural environments and argue common frame-oriented metrics do not directly test this property. [19] VideoPhy evaluates physical interactions using 688 human-verified prompts and reports that the best tested model jointly followed the caption and intuitive physical laws in 19.7% of instances. Its judgments rely on human assessments of intuitive physics rather than exact simulation ground truth. The result cautions against equating short-term visual plausibility with robust physical understanding, while illustrating how benchmark judgments operationalize “physics.” [20]

Safety has a temporal dimension too. T2VSafetyBench assesses text-to-video risks across four categories and 14 aspects, including risks emerging across a sequence—for example, an action or transformation composed of individually benign frames. It samples at one frame per second, a consequential coverage choice. Its authors report that no evaluated system excels in every safety dimension and describe a usability-safety trade-off. Evaluation should therefore consider sequences and disclose its sampling and benchmark scope. [21]

## 5. Synchronized audio-video generation

Joint audio-video generation extends visual temporal coherence to cross-modal event timing, lip synchronization, and correspondence between sound and scene. Audio-Sync Video Generation with Multi-Stream Temporal Control presents an audio-conditioned method and DEMIX, a dataset reported to contain 392,000 clips (1,200 hours) with demixed speech, effects, and music tracks. Its control design distinguishes localized timing, such as lip motion and event onset, from clip-wide visual mood. The authors report gains over compared methods on six metrics, but note limited comparison methods and an evaluation sample of 50 test videos. [22]

VABench proposes evaluation for text-to-audio-video, image-to-audio-video, and stereo audio-video generation across 15 dimensions spanning text-video, text-audio, and video-audio correspondence, synchronization, lip-speech consistency, and audio/video question answering. It argues visual-only metrics cannot assess cross-modal coupling and identifies higher-order physical, emotional, and spatial-audio coherence as underdeveloped evaluation targets. [23] Together, these efforts distinguish generation controls for local event timing and global scene attributes from evaluation of whether sound and image agree in timing and meaning. The evidence base remains emerging, and current claims should be interpreted in light of limited tests and incomplete coverage of cross-modal relations. [22][23]

## Trends and open problems

Several trends emerge. First, latent compression and structured attention address video’s token burden, but compression has fidelity and trainability costs, and attention factorization is a design choice rather than a free reduction. [2][3][5] Second, multimodal sequence models and discrete tokens support task unification, while diffusion and specialized control modules provide alternative approaches to generation and motion/reference constraints. The approaches converge on shared inputs and outputs without converging on one modeling paradigm. [4][7][10][15]

Third, evaluation is broadening from appearance to dynamics, identity, physical interaction, safety, and audio-visual coupling. These dimensions need different benchmarks; no single reported metric captures the full task, and findings remain tied to prompts, model coverage, sampling, and human annotation choices. [17][18][20][21][23]

Open problems include (1) preserving subject and scene identity over long durations while allowing meaningful change; (2) producing controllable and physically credible motion rather than merely smooth clips; (3) combining multiple conditions without one objective degrading another; (4) improving aligned and representative training data while documenting curation; and (5) evaluating audio-video timing and semantics beyond short or narrowly sampled tests. The cited studies address parts of these problems, but do not show all are resolved in a single broadly validated system. [9][16][19][20][22][23]
