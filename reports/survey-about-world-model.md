# World Models: From Latent Dynamics to Generative Interactive Simulators

## TL;DR
- “World model” is an umbrella, not a settled architecture: it can mean a predictive representation, an action-conditioned dynamics model, or a simulator used for planning. Those targets should not be treated as interchangeable. [1][2]
- The durable core of model-based control is learning how state changes and using that model to improve decisions; methods differ in whether they reconstruct observations, predict latent states, or predict only task-relevant values and rewards. [3][4][5][6]
- Generative video models make increasingly compelling and sometimes controllable futures, but visual plausibility is not evidence of causal action fidelity or physical simulation. [7][8][9]
- World models have supported real-robot learning and simulated manipulation planning, yet demonstrations remain task- and setup-specific; transferring from promising model predictions to robust closed-loop behavior is unresolved. [10][11][12]
- Evaluation should combine prediction with interventions, closed-loop task utility, and safety checks: video-quality metrics alone can miss control failures, while task results can confound the model with its planner and data pipeline. [13][14][15]

## Background
A practical definition is a learned internal model of environmental state and change, used to predict consequences of observations and, often, candidate actions for inference, simulation, planning, or control. This formulation spans a range rather than imposing a single criterion: some approaches model future observations, some learn action-conditioned latent transitions, while others retain only decision-relevant quantities. The literature explicitly reflects a tension between “understanding” the world through internal representations and predicting futures for simulation or decision support. [1][2]

The essential test is not whether a system produces a future-looking output, but what it predicts and how that prediction is used. A passive video generator may be temporally coherent without responding correctly to interventions; a compact latent dynamics model may be useful for control without rendering realistic video; and a task-oriented model may support search without reconstructing the environment. [6][7][14]

## Architecture families and the changing meaning of “model”

### From learned transition models to latent imagination
Dyna established a foundational pattern: alternate real interaction with planning from hypothetical experience generated by a learned model. Its model supplies synthetic experience for planning and learning; the model-based RL literature distinguishes such learned-model planning from both model-free learning and planning with known dynamics. [3][2]

The 2018 World Models architecture made the term concrete in pixel-based control: a VAE compresses images, a recurrent MDN-RNN predicts distributions over future latent states conditioned on action and history, and a compact controller acts on the learned features and memory. The authors trained a controller in a generated “dream” environment and transferred it to a task, while explicitly recognizing that a controller can exploit model imperfections. [4]

PlaNet and Dreamer illustrate two different uses of latent dynamics. PlaNet learns a recurrent state-space model (RSSM), combining deterministic recurrent memory with stochastic latent state, then selects actions through online planning in latent space; latent overshooting trains multi-step predictions. Dreamer instead learns an actor-critic from trajectories imagined by its world model, propagating gradients through latent dynamics rather than searching online over action sequences at each step. Thus, world models may serve either as simulators for receding-horizon search or as training grounds for an amortized policy. [5][16]

### Decision-sufficient models and predictive representations
Not every useful world model needs to reproduce sensory reality. MuZero’s learned iterative model predicts reward, value, and policy information for tree search in latent space; it does not need to reconstruct observations or receive the game rules. This task-oriented design contrasts with video simulators: the model is evaluated by whether its latent predictions support planning. [6]

Predictive representation methods add a related but distinct strand. I-JEPA predicts target image representations from context representations rather than reconstructing pixels. V-JEPA 2 similarly uses self-supervised video representation prediction, then post-trains an action-conditioned predictor (V-JEPA 2-AC) on robot interaction data for model-predictive control. This distinction matters: an action-free predictive representation does not by itself specify what an agent’s interventions will cause. [17][18]

These branches expose a design choice rather than a winner: learn detailed observations for visual comparison and imagination, compact latent dynamics for efficient planning, or task-relevant variables for decision-making. The choice changes data needs, computational costs, and what “accuracy” means. The broad survey literature likewise treats the definition as unsettled, not reducible to one model family. [2][1]

## World models for robotics and embodied control

### Latent models trained through interaction
Physical-robot learning is a particularly demanding test because data are costly and model errors can affect real actions. DayDreamer applies Dreamer online on four physical robots, learning from real interaction without a simulator. Its demonstrations include quadruped standing and walking, adaptation to pushes, sparse-reward arm pick-and-place, and camera-based wheeled navigation. These results show feasibility in named tasks, not general deployment; the authors identify accurate real-world model learning as an open challenge. [10]

V-JEPA 2 separates broad video pretraining from action grounding: the paper reports that its action-conditioned V-JEPA 2-AC was post-trained on robot interaction, then used for image-goal MPC. It reports zero-shot Franka manipulation deployments in two labs after less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot interaction video, without task-specific reward or training in those new labs. The authors’ own distinction reinforces a general lesson: passive visual prediction alone does not identify causal effects of the agent’s actions. [18]

### Action-conditioned video as a planning interface
Video world models retain visual futures as an interface to planning. iVideoGPT predicts tokenized sequences of observations, actions, and rewards and is evaluated on action-conditioned video prediction, visual MPC, and model-based RL; its authors report that broad pretraining is especially useful when downstream data are scarce. RoboDreamer decomposes language into action and object-relation primitives, generates video plans, then maps adjacent generated frames to actions through an inverse-dynamics policy. Its reported robot execution is in simulation, and its compositional generalization is conditioned on primitives being in-distribution. [12][11]

Benchmarks make clear why the interface matters. VP² scores action-conditioned video predictors by downstream manipulation success under sampling-based visual MPC and replanning. Across five tested predictors, common perceptual measures such as FVD, LPIPS, and SSIM did not consistently predict control success, with correlations varying by task. This is a concrete warning against selecting a planner’s model solely by video appearance. [13]

## Generative video and multimodal world models

### From plausible futures to controllable environments
Large video generators have popularized a visual conception of world modeling. Sora’s technical report describes a diffusion transformer that generates video from spacetime patches in compressed latents, and notes suggestive behaviors such as persistent paint strokes and prompted Minecraft-like control. But the same report documents errors in basic physics and state change, long-video incoherence, and spontaneous object appearances; it does not disclose full model and implementation details. The authors’ own evidence therefore supports an ambitious generator with notable failure modes, not a validated general simulator. [7]

Interactive models add an action channel. Genie learns gameplay-like interactive environments from unlabeled Internet video, combining a video tokenizer, autoregressive dynamics model, and unsupervised latent actions; users control generated worlds frame by frame through those latent actions. This makes an important conceptual advance over passive generation, but inferred latent controls are not necessarily calibrated, semantically specified real-world actions. [8]

Driving and other multimodal systems introduce text and control signals alongside video. GAIA-1 uses video, text, and vehicle actions in discrete-token sequence modeling and conditions predicted futures on ego actions, speed, curvature, or text. The paper reports contextual and geometry-related representations, while noting autoregressive generation is not real-time; the reported generated scenarios should not be equated with evidence of safe real-world driving prediction. [19]

### Physics, geometry, and action fidelity
A recurring research move is to correct what generic video generation leaves unspecified. VideoPhy evaluates prompted material interactions and found that even the strongest tested model met both caption and physical-law criteria in only 39.6% of human-evaluated instances; this result is benchmark- and snapshot-specific, but directly illustrates the gap between prompt adherence and physical commonsense. [9]

Other lines add explicit action or spatial structure. IRASim conditions diffusion on historical observations and frame-level robot actions to align predicted frames with robot motion; its reported uses include simulator-based policy evaluation and test-time planning, not demonstrated physical closed-loop success. FantasyWorld adds a geometric branch to predict video latents alongside an implicit 3D field, targeting multiview coherence; geometric consistency, however, is not itself action-conditioned physical dynamics. [20]

The design spectrum is therefore widening: better video rendering, latent action control, multimodal conditioning, and explicit geometry address different deficiencies. A visually detailed model can still mispredict interventions; a geometrically coherent model can still lack causal dynamics; and a model with an action input can still fail to preserve the consequences of that action over long horizons. [8][19][7][20]

## Evaluation: prediction is not utility

### Measure the property a model is claimed to provide
Evaluation needs to match the claim. Video metrics measure visual or temporal similarity, but do not necessarily test whether actions have the correct consequences. VP² finds these metrics inconsistently associated with downstream manipulation performance. WorldModelBench adds instruction following and physics adherence to video assessment, making explicit that visual quality alone does not capture all properties relevant to a world-model claim. [13][14]

A different class of benchmarks tests causal and procedural understanding. WorldPrediction uses initial and final states, action transitions, and counterfactual distractors to test action matching and planning. On its human-filtered examples, the abstract reports frontier-model accuracy up to 57% on its world-modeling test and 38% on procedural planning, compared with perfect human performance on retained examples. These are discriminative tests rather than closed-loop robot experiments, but they reveal capabilities obscured by image quality. [21]

### Closed-loop utility, safety, and attribution
For an embodied decision-making claim, more direct tests include intervention fidelity, closed-loop rollout validity, reward/value prediction, policy ranking, optimization lift, exploitability, and uncertainty calibration. A safety-focused survey distinguishes agent evaluation (for example, environment reward or driving incidents) from generated-output metrics, and identifies hallucination and inadequate uncertainty evaluation as concerns. [15]

A final task score alone may not isolate the world model’s contribution: the optimizer, reward signal, training data, and execution policy can all contribute. Conversely, good prediction scores may conceal a model that supports poor action selection. This motivates staged evaluation: test action-conditioned prediction, compare fixed-policy rankings against reality, assess closed-loop task outcomes, and measure whether model-optimized policies exploit model errors. The available evaluation literature identifies these as important gaps rather than settled standard practice. [13][14][15]

## Trends and open problems

1. **From passive prediction to intervention-aware modeling.** Action-free video pretraining is useful as representation learning, but interactive planning requires models that respond to candidate actions. The shift from V-JEPA 2 pretraining to action-conditioned post-training, and from passive video generators to systems such as Genie, exemplifies this boundary. The unresolved issue is whether action conditioning remains causally reliable under novel actions and states. [18][8]
2. **Choosing the right abstraction.** Pixel/video simulation offers inspectable outputs, latent-state models can be efficient for planning, and task-oriented models can avoid reconstructing irrelevant detail. No single target dominates across perception, control, and compute constraints. Future systems need explicit clarity about what state variables and consequences they preserve. [5][6][12]
3. **Long-horizon consistency and compounding error.** Every autoregressive rollout risks drift; video generation reports include temporal incoherence, while interactive models must also preserve objects, geometry, and action effects as the horizon grows. Reliable uncertainty estimates and mechanisms to correct a rollout against observed reality are important research needs. [7][15]
4. **A task-facing evaluation stack.** Current benchmarks cover distinct slices—video quality and physics adherence, action-transition reasoning, visual MPC, and robot task success—rather than one universally accepted test. A stronger practice should combine these levels and report costs, uncertainty, transfer conditions, and matched baselines. [13][14][21]
5. **Safety and model exploitation.** Controllers optimized inside an imperfect learned model can exploit its errors, a concern visible from early dream-environment training through current safety discussions. For high-stakes embodied use, validation must include out-of-distribution actions, model uncertainty, failure detection, and real-world verification; generated plausibility cannot substitute for these checks. [4][15]

The field’s central question is shifting from “Can a model predict or generate a plausible future?” to “Can it predict the counterfactual consequences that matter, under intervention, for long enough to improve decisions safely?” Progress will depend on both better modeling and more rigorous evidence about when its predictions are decision-useful. [1][13][15]
